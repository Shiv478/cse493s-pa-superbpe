Parity-Aware SuperBPE: Reducing Cross-Lingual Compression
Disparities in Two-Stage Tokenizers
Chendur Jayavelu, Shiv Vinodshankar, Dhruti Vadlamudi
Paul G. Allen School of Computer Science & Engineering, University of Washington
{chendurj, shivvin, dhrutiv}@cs.washington.edu
1 Introduction
Byte-pair encoding (BPE) tokenizers are trained by maximizing global compression on a training corpus. When that
corpus is dominated by one language — as virtually every web-scale pretraining mix is by English — the resulting
vocabulary compresses the majority language efficiently and fragments under-represented languages into far more tokens
per character. Petrov et al. (2023) and Ahia et al. (2023) document max/min disparities of 5–15× in tokens-per-character
across languages for production tokenizers (e.g. GPT-2 on our own FLORES-200 measurement has a Gini of 0.348 and
a max/min of 9.6×). This disparity is not merely cosmetic: it inflates inference cost, shrinks effective context windows,
and degrades downstream task performance for the affected languages.
SuperBPE(Liu et al., 2024) extends BPE with a two-stage curriculum: stage 1 applies a GPT-4-style regex pre-
tokenizer that splits on whitespace and Unicode category boundaries; stage 2 “lifts whitespace” by dropping the
whitespace constraint from the regex and learning additional merges that can cross word boundaries. SuperBPE
achieves substantially better English compression than vanilla BPE at the same vocabulary size, but does so using the
same global-frequency merge-selection rule as BPE, and its evaluation is English-centric. The interaction between
SuperBPE’s whitespace-lifting and multilingual disparity has not been studied.
Parity-aware BPE(Foroutan et al., 2024) modifies the merge-selection rule itself: at each step, identify the language
with the worst current compression and select the merge that most reduces that language’s symbol count. The vendored
implementation (parity_aware_bpe) supports continuation from preloaded merges, which we exploit below.
Our workcomposes these two ideas. We hypothesize that SuperBPE’s English compression gains widen the cross-
lingual gap, and that applying parity-aware merge selectionwithin each of SuperBPE’s two stagescan close the gap
while preserving most of the compression benefit. This Milestone 1 (V1) report focuses on the first half of that claim —
characterizing the disparity vanilla SuperBPE produces relative to vanilla BPE — and on validating the implementation
that the remaining hypotheses will use. We refer to our combined method asPA-SuperBPE.
2 Scope of the Project
This is original research that builds on two existing code bases (SuperBPE and parity-aware BPE) and tests three
falsifiable hypotheses about how tokenizer training objectives interact with multilingual compression disparity. The
disparity metric throughout is theGini coefficient over per-language mean tokens-per-character (Unicode code
points), measured on the FLORES-200devtest split (1,012 multi-parallel sentences). We also report the per-language
max/min ratio for interpretability. Lower Gini denotes more equitable compression across languages.

3 Methodology
3.1 Algorithm: PA-SuperBPE
PA-SuperBPE composes SuperBPE’s two-stage curriculum with parity-aware merge selection. Both stages use the
parity-aware learner of Foroutan et al.; the stages differ only in their pre-tokenizer and a stage-2-only merge filter.
Pre-tokenization.We reproduce SuperBPE’s exact HuggingFace pipeline, Split(Regex(...),
behavior="isolated") + ByteLevel(use_regex=False), with two different regexes:
• Stage 1: GPT-4-style regex that splits on whitespace and Unicode category boundaries (no token crosses
whitespace).
• Stage 2: looser regex that drops the whitespace boundary, allowing merges that span multiple whitespace-
segmented words.
Because the upstream parity-aware learner only shipswhitespace andbytelevel pre-tokenizers, we run SuperBPE’s
pipeline ourselves in Python and feed the resulting pretoken frequency dictionaries to the learner via its –dict-input
interface. This required no modifications to either vendored repository.

Parity dev set.The per-language symbol counts ℓt[l] are computed over the FLORES-200 dev split (997 multi-

than UTF-8 bytes, since byte counts triple-charge non-Latin scripts and would obscure the disparity we are trying to
characterize.
Stage transition.Stage 1 emits a merges.txt. Stage 2 invokes the same learner with–preload merges.txtand
the stage-2 pretokenizer, which replays the stage-1 merges over the stage-2-segmented corpus and then continues
with parity-aware selection until the total vocabulary is reached. We additionally enforce SuperBPE’s stage-2 filters:
candidate merges that produce a token containing:˙G, or that span more than four˙G-words, are vetoed and re-selected.
These filters apply only in stage 2 (in stage 1 they are vacuous because the regex disallows whitespace inside pretokens).
We default Foroutan’s–global-merges(a global-frequency warmup knob) to 0 in both stages; sweeping it is a
deferred V2 study.

The pipeline lives inpa_superbpe/ (3 Python modules totalling ∼260 lines): pretokenize.py (SuperBPE-regex
pretokenizer → per-language dict files), learner.py (wrapper that injects the stage-2 :˙G/4-word filter into the
parity-aware learner’s inner loop), andpipeline.py (stage 1 → stage 2 → HF tokenizer assembly). Vendored code
in vendor/superbpe/ and vendor/parity-aware-bpe/ is unmodified; the final tokenizer is assembled into a

3.3 Data
Tokenizer training (full).A 5–10 GB subsample of the OLMo-2 Mix, language-balanced to the extent the source
allows. The data/prepare_corpus.py script handles streaming, deduplication, and per-language sharding. Baseline
tokenizers (BPE-40k, BPE-200k, SuperBPE-40k, SuperBPE-200k) and the shared corpus are owned by Shiv; the
multilingual evaluation harness and PA-SuperBPE implementation are owned by Chendur.
Tokenizer training (V1 smoke).A 50 MB subsample across {eng_Latn, spa_Latn, zho_Hans, hin_Deva,
tha_Thai} with vocab sizes 8k stage-1 / 16k total. This was used purely to validate the pipeline end-to-end be-
fore the full multilingual corpus was ready.
Evaluation.FLORES-200 devtest split (1,012 parallel sentences) restricted to 10 typologically diverse languages:
eng_Latn, spa_Latn, deu_Latn, rus_Cyrl, arb_Arab, hin_Deva, zho_Hans, jpn_Jpan, tha_Thai, swh_Latn.
For H3, we will additionally evaluate FLORES perplexity and XNLI accuracy on the trained LM.
3.4 Hyperparameters and Experimental Setup
For each of the four tokenizer methods (BPE, SuperBPE, parity-aware BPE single-stage, PA-SuperBPE), at each of two
vocabulary sizes (40k, 200k), we train one tokenizer on the shared OLMo-2 subsample and evaluate it on FLORES-200
devtest. Following SuperBPE’s released configuration, the two-stage methods use an 80k stage-1 / 120k stage-2 split at
vocab=200k (and a proportional 16k/24k split at vocab=40k). Minimum merge frequency is 2. Parity-aware methods
usebasevariant,–global-merges 0, and FLORES dev as the parity reference.
For each tokenizer we compute, per language,total_chars,total_tokens, andmean_tokens_per_char; we then
compute the Gini coefficient and max/min ratio across the 10-language vector. Comparison tables are emitted by
eval/aggregate.py.
For H3 (post-V1), each tokenizer is used to pretrain a ∼150M-parameter decoder-only LM on 2–5 B tokens of the
OLMo-2 Mix on Lightning AI, evaluated by FLORES per-language perplexity and XNLI accuracy.
3.5 Code and Compute
Code.Two open-source repositories are vendored unchanged: SuperBPE (Liu et al.) and parity-aware BPE (Foroutan
et al.). PA-SuperBPE is approximately 260 lines of new Python ( ∼80 lines pretokenizer, ∼60 lines stage-2 filter
wrapper, ∼120 lines pipeline orchestration), plus the multilingual evaluation harness (∼300 lines undereval/). The
HF tokenizer assembly reuses Foroutan’sbuild_vocab_from_mergesutility.
Compute.Tokenizer training is CPU-bound: the V1 smoke run (50 MB, 16k merges) completes in under 10 minutes
on a local workstation; the full BPE/SuperBPE/PA-SuperBPE runs at vocab=200k on the 5–10 GB corpus take a few
hours each on a Lightning AI Studio. Evaluation across 10 languages on FLORES devtest takes seconds per tokenizer.
Significant compute is required only for H3: pretraining four 150M-parameter LMs on 2–5 B tokens each, budgeted on
Lightning AI GPUs (rough estimate: 100–200 GPU-hours per LM on a single H100, total ∼0.5–1k GPU-hours across
the four conditions).
